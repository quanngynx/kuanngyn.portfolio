# Chapter 1: Preparation and Behavioral Questions

## Core Idea

Preparation should create retrieval-ready evidence: technical fundamentals you can implement, project stories you can explain under probing, and a feedback loop based on observed mistakes.

## Preparation Priorities

1. Implement core structures and algorithms without relying on autocomplete.
2. Solve problems before reading solutions; use hints progressively.
3. Write and test code on paper or a constrained editor.
4. Type the unchanged draft into a compiler and log every defect.
5. Conduct narrated mock interviews.
6. Revisit weaknesses through spaced, mixed practice.

Reading solutions creates familiarity, not production skill. Measure whether you can recognize the shape, derive a baseline, optimize, implement, test, and explain.

## Project Story Inventory

Prepare several projects with evidence for:

| Question | Evidence to prepare |
| --- | --- |
| What problem existed? | User/business impact and constraints |
| What did you own? | Personal decisions and boundaries, distinct from team work |
| What alternatives existed? | Trade-offs and rejected options |
| What failed? | Concrete symptom, diagnosis, and correction |
| What result occurred? | Measured outcome or honest qualitative evidence |
| What would you change? | Limitation and next improvement |

Know architecture, difficult bugs, data flow, testing, scale assumptions, and why major technologies were chosen. Interviewers may probe beyond a rehearsed summary.

## Behavioral Response Structure

Use a compact situation-action-result-reflection structure:

1. **Context**: enough detail to understand stakes and constraints.
2. **Responsibility**: what you personally owned.
3. **Decision and action**: what you did and why.
4. **Result**: evidence without invented metrics.
5. **Reflection**: trade-off, lesson, or improvement.

The action should dominate the answer. Avoid a long company history followed by “we fixed it.”

## “Tell Me About Yourself” Router

Build a two-minute narrative:

- present role or technical identity;
- two or three relevant experiences connected by a theme;
- evidence of fit for the target role;
- what you want next.

Do not recite the resume chronologically. Select details that predict performance in this role.

## Resume and Experience Signals

- Prefer accomplishments and decisions over duty lists.
- Separate implemented outcomes from proposals and experiments.
- Quantify only when the metric is real and attributable.
- Include technologies as evidence of decisions, not as an inventory.
- Be prepared to explain every claim and term.
- Remove vague expertise labels you cannot defend under follow-up.

## Mock Interview Design

Rotate roles: candidate, interviewer, observer. The observer records:

- time to clarify and produce a baseline;
- assumptions left implicit;
- complexity errors;
- silent intervals;
- hints needed and how they were used;
- defects found before versus after execution.

Review the process before reviewing the final answer.

## Common Mistakes

- Memorizing polished stories with no technical depth.
- Taking credit for team outcomes without ownership clarity.
- Inventing production scale or metrics.
- Practicing only favorite topics.
- Reading a solution immediately after getting stuck.
- Using mock interviews as performances instead of diagnostic sessions.
- Failing to prepare questions for the interviewer about real work and expectations.

## Representative Preparation Plan

```text
Day A: one structure implementation + two focused problems
Day B: one mixed timed problem + defect-log review
Day C: project deep dive + behavioral story rehearsal
Day D: narrated mock interview + targeted remediation
Weekly: reattempt one previously failed problem from a blank page
```

Adjust the plan from evidence. If implementation is sound but recognition is slow, increase mixed routing practice. If ideas are good but code fails, prioritize paper coding and tests.
